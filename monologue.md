## 1 · Why the Scientific Revolution didn't happen sooner

*Source: e3 · Three Pillars of Science (Resources · Curiosity · Precision)*
*Pass: 2 — rearrangement (your sentences from Pass 1, reordered; ~20 connector words added).*

The History of Science is litered with incredible people discovering ideas and technologies millenia ahead of its time, for some reason never causing a cascade of revolutions such as we saw starting in the 16th century. Why? Maybe it didn't reach a critical point of contradictory observation up to that point. However, simply looking at science itself without considering the human nature is probably unwise. 

A scientist in ancient rome might have the perfect scientific mindset such as that of Aristotle and even make the realisations about cosmos millenia ahead like Aristarcus is meaningless withou the percision of mathematical models and optical tools to measure those stars. Imagination without mathematics is blurry like a dream. Hard to convince anyone its true.

Second, a scientist in the ancient times might have discovered quantum mechanics and complex numbers all by himslef, but he would still at some point die. Without a legacy that can be written, copied, remembered, understood ideas die along with the people who invented them. its hard to remember something that is beyond your understanding or believe somethign that is beyone your time. discoveries have to come at the right time and be protected and spread.

 needs three pillars. The first is people and physical resources. The second is mental resources — curiosity, abstraction, and ideology. The third is precision — mathematics and tools.

The mental pillar starts with abstraction. You need the ability to abstract — to consider ideas in a more general way — because that lets you use those ideas across different fields. You cannot find generalised truths without combining ideas from different frameworks. In the past, many disciplines had good scientific thinking — rules, analytical explanations — but those discoveries stayed inside their fields. If you're an expert metalworker who builds good abstract rules about metals, you won't automatically apply them to woodworking or alchemy. You need a critical mass of science being done across multiple fields at once before you can compare the discoveries. In the past, either not enough were made to reveal the connections, or there weren't enough people working across fields.

Math was the exception. Math is the easiest connector to spot, which is why people say it reflects the beauty of nature — because it's everywhere. You can instantly recognise that the math is the same. The laws of physics and chemistry are everywhere too, but they're much harder to notice.

Abstract thinking also needs liberty of thought, which is hard when you have hard constraints. Religion is the hardest mental constraint there is. Whenever science threatened to progress, some aspect of religion pushed it back. I'm not saying religion was bad — it served the important purpose of unifying moral values. But we have to be careful: too secular or too dismissive an approach won't go well in the Lithuanian education system. The breakthroughs came from thinkers brave enough to consider ideas that were not accepted — uncommon, sometimes taboo. The people who allowed themselves to analyse despite the social pressure of divine authority, witchcraft accusations, or taboo subjects. If you believe God created everything and nothing remains to be discovered, you'll be discouraged from trying.

The third pillar is precision. The difference between Plato and Aristotle is that Plato thought everything could be worked out in the mind; Aristotle went to check. That's more precise. Saying *all animals have wings* is less precise than checking and saying *the twenty birds I saw do indeed have wings*. Saying *if you push a rock, it moves* is less precise than *if you push a rock with the force of two horses, it will move twenty metres in twenty seconds*. Saying *sulfuric acid dissolves metal* is less precise than *a solution of twenty percent water and eighty percent sulfuric acid dissolves this metal*. Any tools, any measurements, any math is precision.

Without it — as Richard Behiel put it — mathematics cannot sharpen your imagination to the next level.

## 2 · Discrete vs continuous, and the spherical cow

*Source: e6 · Discrete vs Continuous thinking*

There's another important part of science: discrete thinking. You can think about thinking in two ways — continuous and discrete. Continuous thinking says there's an infinite amount of different kinds of sciences, objects, thoughts, and precisions. Discrete thinking says everything is made of smallest, indivisible parts; find the smallest parts and you can construct the larger ones from them.

This isn't to say continuous thinking is wrong, or that continuous objects don't exist. Fields in physics don't necessarily have a smallest part — we still think of them as continuous. But materials used to be thought of as continuous too, until people started treating them as discrete, as having smallest chunks. The same applies to thinking itself: you can treat ideas as a continuum, or you can try to find the smallest atoms of ideas and build up from there.

This is related to empiricism — the requirement to check. Empiricism matters, though I'm not yet sure exactly how it combines with the rest.

Then there's Sean Carroll's spherical cow. This may be the fourth or fifth column of science — we're still working it out. The spherical cow approximation is just that approximations and idealisations work in physics. In other fields, they don't. Generalisations let you create rules, and then you can search for the rules that never change. Even if the rules themselves change, you can define meta-rules that describe how they change.

You can think of this as a clockwork universe — everything predictable. The state now lets you predict the future. As I understand it, that's not how people thought of it in the past. They saw tendencies and properties; they used math to describe rather than predict. I'm not yet sure how to explain this, or whether it's even true, but there might be a mental shift toward predictions, idealisations, approximations, and generalisations. That shift is related to the second column of science, and to discrete-versus-continuous thinking, which searches for the atoms of rules and atoms of thoughts so we can build everything else from them.

> **What I changed**
> Removed the *On top of all of this, I would like to add* runway. Cut *which is comprised of*. Compressed *I'm not quite sure how to explain this yet or if this is true* into a single hedge. Smoothed *I'm not sure how it all combines with this, but it's related* into one clause.

## 3 · The Natural Science Boundary

*Source: e4 · Natural Science Boundary*

The last piece I want to mention is natural science — the idea that allowed abstract thought to break free from religion. The name *natural science* is deliberate: it identifies that we are discussing things that are not supernatural. Even if there are unexplainable things, higher powers, and matters beyond our comprehension — and I think this is probably the most crucial idea ever to emerge in human history — there are also things that are knowable and explainable. It is a good idea to focus only on the explainable and knowable, expand that field, and leave the unexplained and unknowable in a different supernatural field.

The idea of natural science is that you can ignore all the things you don't understand and focus on the things you do. To put it more precisely: you can ignore things that are never understandable, and ignore things that are never knowable, because they're never going to help you. You may as well focus on what you can know and understand. That way, you expand the area you can know and understand. After two thousand years of this, people realise they can know and understand everything — and that supernatural things don't exist. Or maybe they do, but it's inconsequential, because we can simply ignore them and see how far we get with the explainable.

> **What I changed**
> Eliminated three uses of *idea* in the opening sentence. Cut the self-correcting interjection (*okay, that's not how I wanted it to sound. I mean…*) and folded its actual content into the surrounding paragraph. Replaced *just try* with *try*, *just focus* with *focus*.

## 5 · The deep forest approach to science education

*Source: e12 · The Deep Forest (long pedagogy monologue covering deep forest, exams, language analogy, intuition before precision)*

Not Feynman style. It will be my style — a combination of all the YouTube resources I know and like. A mix of the best teachers on YouTube, but with the overall narrative — the path between them — written by me. I want to add Yuval Harari's introduction to the progression of science, and Sean Carroll's *Biggest Ideas in the Universe*.

Even starting in fifth grade, I would not hesitate to tell students about quarks and quark stars. That's the inspiring material that keeps them asking questions. And to understand something as amazing as a quark star, a Planck star, or how a solid thing can actually be a wave — where two identical waves push each other while other waves don't — you have to start with something simpler. Once children understand the *purpose* of learning the simple things, they don't resist learning them with the high accuracy exams demand. Exams demand that you explain simple things with very high accuracy.

It's the same as language learning. Exams usually require you to know specific grammar rules. That's fine — it's hard to measure how good a student is if you can't measure them specifically, so it makes sense that exams are specific. But it's not the best way to learn. Just as memorising grammar rules and vocabulary is a terrible way to become a good language learner — because language is mostly automatic. Intuitive. You need thousands of examples and thousands of hours of exposure before you can vaguely understand or describe something. But whatever you can describe will sound natural, intuitive, and fast.

For physics, I suggest the deep forest approach — the same intuitive, high-input method Stephen Krashen uses for languages — so the student builds a real intuition for physics. Only once you have a decent intuition, just as in language learning, can you ask specific questions to solve specific problems. The context allows you to memorise those specific solutions far better.

In language learning, this means you understand the language more or less, but you don't know how a word is spelled or the correct order of the words. You know examples of people saying these things, but you can't quite remember. Then you find a rule, and you remember. In physics it's the same: you learn the general understanding of how physics works, what matter is, what the forces are, how they interact. Then you can ask the specific mathematical question — how much of *this* makes *that*, and what energy is in specific units.

Smoothed the *maybe that's not the most inspiring thing, but you can think of a lot of inspiring things* aside without losing the point. Standardised *Steven* → *Stephen* Krashen. Preserved every analogy.

## 6 · Math as intuition first

*Source: e13 · Math intuition (continuation of the same Deep Forest monologue)*

I would teach math the same way. It might sound absurd that you can teach math abstractly and intuitively, but math is closely related to physics. Many math rules are simply listed, memorised, and practiced. I don't disagree with practicing math — most algebra can be understood quickly, but you need extreme accuracy and speed with algebra for most of math and physics. That requires pure practice. But a lot of that algebra has real discussions to be had. What is a negative sign? Is the number line the only way to look at it? How are zero and infinity the same? How do circular and linear geometries show similarities and differences between zero, infinity, and circular motion? What does it mean to raise something to a power? How can you decompose that into rotation and stretching? What is multiplication — why is it more natural in multiple dimensions?

For example: two by two equals four. That makes sense — you stretch it twice. Two metres times two metres gives four square metres — also natural; you stretch by two, you get four. But measure the same line in different units — two thousand millimetres times two thousand millimetres — and multiplication suddenly stops fitting on a line. You get four trillion square millimetres on a line, which is absurd. Expand it into an area instead and it looks natural: two thousand by two thousand makes a clean square. Squeeze it onto a line and the smaller your units, the more infinitely long the number becomes. The initial choice of units determines the length of the number, which is not symmetrical. Multiplication is only symmetrical in planes — not on lines.

The same questions can be asked about cosine and sine, or about what it means to raise to a power — every one of them can be discussed and analysed. Instead of just listing a thousand rules: one of the rules that surprised me in school — how to multiply something like (a + b)² — turns out, if you square it, to reveal Pascal's triangle. That triangle appears all over math, but it's never mentioned in school. And once you see it, you can deduce the values logically; you don't need to memorise the rule.

Same with the quadratic formula. They give you the discriminant without explaining where it comes from. I've had a lot of fun trying to reinvent it. What I discovered was completing the square — turning ax² + bx + c into a perfect square makes everything easier. It connects Pascal's triangle, the (a + b)² expansion, and the derivative of a quadratic function. Connect all of those and you see why each one is useful to the others. You can ask deeper questions, like why x² expands the way it does — and you'll get the same answer as the multiplication question: the square of a number isn't natural on a line. If a quadratic equation were portrayed in a more natural environment, you would see that it grows normally.

Same with derivatives. They just give you the rules. But if you analyse a little, you can visualise a derivative as the rate of change of an area. To find the rate of change of a square, what's actually changing is the two sides. However you grow the square, it grows at twice the speed, because it has two sides. So the derivative of x² is 2x — and you derived it from geometry, not from a rule.

Replaced *they give you the determinant* (the original meant *discriminant*) — kept the original word in *braindump/index.html* but corrected here. Standardised x² and (a + b)² formatting.

## 7 · Memorisation — its role and its limits

*Source: e14 · Memorization (continuation of the same Deep Forest monologue)*

This is just to show that mathematics can also be explained intuitively. Only once you have the intuition — and I'm not against memorisation. But memorisation doesn't work long-term. Subjects like Japanese, and fields like medicine, use tools like Anki for brutal memorisation. I've used Anki myself. I'm familiar with memorisation techniques and modern memorisation tools. I know you can remember something indefinitely if you space it out properly.

But in the end, your natural spaced repetition is your exposure to the field. Most memorisation works as hooks. Either you don't need spaced repetition because you'll get the information naturally once you encounter the subject in real life — but once you do encounter it, you need to be able to remember it so you can recognise it and retrieve it in context. That's why, initially, when you don't yet have enough context, rote memorisation can be a great way to create hooks that let you notice things.

It's the same Stephen Krashen idea about noticing — a European can't learn the pitch accent of Japanese but can learn the tones of Chinese. In Chinese, pitch is necessary; in Japanese, it isn't. Because it isn't necessary, you have to add extra hooks to notice it. Once you add the hooks, you can notice. But if the information has no use, it dies off. If you think the information is genuinely useful and you're afraid it'll die off, the long-term solution is to analyse the subject in a more complex way — because that generates natural contextual hooks instead of artificial memorisation-technique ones.

> **What I changed**
> Standardised *memorization* → *memorisation* (matches *analyse*, *recognise*). Cut *basically*. Tightened the *Either you don't have to do spaced repetition because you'll get it naturally* construction so the *Either…but* clause reads as one thought. Final phrase preserved verbatim — *natural contextual hooks instead of artificial memorisation-technique ones* is the line worth keeping.

## 8 · Deep forest — going in first, understanding later

*Source: e12 · The standalone "deep forest" moment*

You go into the deep forest, see amazing things, and try to understand very complicated ones. You can get the idea. You don't need specifics — you can get the idea. You can answer some big questions, finding safe spots in the deep, dark forest.

With the deep forest, you don't have to dumb it down. You can show people that quantum mechanics and special relativity exist and change things, without explaining everything. That's the problem with the modern school curriculum: it's so oriented towards exams that it's afraid to even mention anything unless it can be fully described and explained. The student is left with information that's neither explained nor consistent. The only option left is to memorise everything — which isn't very interesting, which is why students don't want to learn.

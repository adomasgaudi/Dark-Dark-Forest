The foundational era, drawn in time

All sciences · 300 years · scrollable and zoomable timeline

The three hundred years from Galileo's telescope (1610) to J.J. Thomson's electron (1897) are the foundation everything else stands on. Physics got Newton, then Maxwell. Chemistry got Lavoisier, Dalton, Mendeleev. Biology got cells, Darwin, Mendel, Pasteur. Mathematics got calculus, non-Euclidean geometry, the manifold concept Einstein would later need. By 1900, every major science had a working core, even if the deepest puzzles (what is matter, what is life, what is light) were still open.

The chart below is the same proportional Gantt as the other tables, but interactive. The 1840s through 1890s are too dense to read at full view — too many discoveries packed into too few decades. So you can zoom in. scroll to zoom at the cursor. click + drag to pan. The buttons below the chart do the same.

Why this era looks busy

Notice the density. At default zoom the second half of the 1800s is unreadable — too many overlapping dots. The 1860s alone contain Maxwell's equations, the periodic table, Darwin's Origin, Pasteur's germ theory, Mendel's genetics, and Riemann's geometric foundations. Five separate sciences crossed major thresholds in a single decade.

This is what the pre-1600 chart doesn't have. Most rows there run the full length without much change. The scientific revolution doesn't just solve things faster — it solves them at a rate the chart itself struggles to display.

By 1900 the table looks roughly complete to anyone reading it. Then the modern physics chart opens with a near-instant aftershock — radioactivity (1896), the electron (1897), the quantum (1900), relativity (1905). The "complete" picture turned out to be the surface of something deeper, and the cycle restarted.

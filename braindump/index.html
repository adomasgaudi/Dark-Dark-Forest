Discrete vs Continuous thinking ▶
  • Continuous: infinite gradation, no smallest part
  • Discrete: everything composed of smallest indivisible units — "atoms of ideas"
  • Find the atoms → build everything else up from them
  • Matter went from continuous to atomic; fields are still continuous
  • Same shift applies to thinking itself — atomic ideas, compose upward

Why no earlier Scientific Revolution — Harari + Carroll ▶
  • Harari: Scientific Revolution = revolution of ignorance, not knowledge
  • Carroll (math): Algebra, calculus, telescope, pendulum clock all had to exist
  • Two phases: Descriptive (1500–1665), Unifying (1665+)
  • Bottleneck was cultural, not mathematical — math was ready by 1300
  • Carroll (criticality): sandpile model — science crossed self-organized criticality ~1665
  • Islamic Golden Age = poignant near-miss, ~70% of ingredients
▶ In depth · synthesis
  • Harari: No civilization before ~1500 admitted ignorance. Sacred texts claimed the important things were already known. The willingness to say "I don't know" was the precondition
  • Carroll (math): Algebra (820 CE), calculus (1665), telescope (1608), pendulum clock (1657) — modern physics needs both the symbolic language and the measurement tools
  • Two phases: Descriptive (1500–1665) finds patterns without deriving them. Unifying (1665+) — Newton shows Kepler's 3 laws follow from one force equation
  • Cultural bottleneck: printing press + Reformation + colonial funding converged 1450–1520. The grains were available earlier; the pile wasn't
  • Sandpile dynamics: add grains one at a time; nothing happens for a long time, then a single grain triggers a total avalanche. Self-organized criticality is the right metaphor for how science "ignited"
  • Islamic Golden Age: had ~70% of ingredients. Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258) removed grains, pile relaxed back to subcritical
Core thesis

The tipping point isn't a year — it's the first moment all ingredients are simultaneously strong. Harari explains what gets grains onto the pile. Carroll explains what happens once criticality is reached.

Key example
Kerala school — the sharpest argument in the framework ▶
  • Madhava had Taylor-series-equivalent math by ~1400 CE — 250 years before Newton
  • It didn't cascade — no printing press, no funding loop, no critical density
  • Stayed regional, written in Sanskrit and Malayalam, nearly lost
  • Better question: "why did Kerala NOT trigger a revolution in India?"
  • Proves: math is necessary but not sufficient. The social arguments do the real work
▶ In depth · the historical fact
Madhava of Sangamagrama developed infinite series for sin, cos, arctan — including what we now call the Taylor series — around 1400 CE, roughly 250 years before Newton and Leibniz.
  • Madhava's school continued for ~200 years after him (Nilakantha, Jyeshthadeva) without triggering broader scientific takeoff
  • The work was rediscovered by Western historians of science only in the 20th century
  • This is the cleanest counterexample to "math is the bottleneck" — the math was there and it sat for 2 centuries
Why it matters for the curriculum

Kerala is the killer example for the sandpile / critical-mass argument. Use it whenever a student asks "why didn't this happen earlier?" — because someone did have the math, and nothing happened.

Math teaching
Matrix intuition — column view vs row view ▶
  • Row view (textbook): dot each row with the input vector
  • Column view (deeper): each column is a basis direction; input = weights
  • Both give identical results — column view is conceptually richer
  • Always think of M·x as a linear combination of columns
  • Matrices reveal when a question is unsolvable in your current space
▶ In depth · pedagogy
  • Row view origin: came from solving systems of equations — each row IS one equation. The convention crystallized with Cayley (1858), which is why textbooks still lead with it
  • Column view origin: each column is a "food profile" or basis direction. Input vector = how much of each column. Output = the weighted sum
  • The nutrient example: if columns are foods (their nutrient profiles), then M·x asks "if I eat these quantities, what nutrients do I get?" — the input lives in food-space, the output in nutrient-space
  • The negative-yogurt trick: the inverse problem ("I want exactly these nutrients, what do I eat?") sometimes requires negative quantities of yogurt. Matrices reveal that your target is outside the span — same geometry that surfaces in eigenvectors and null spaces

Core pedagogy · Verbatim
The Deep Forest — pedagogy core ▶
  • Go into the deep forest first — amazing things, complicated ideas, big questions
  • You don't have to dumb it down; you have to not explain everything
  • Even Grade 5 can encounter quark stars, wave-particle duality, special relativity
  • The inspiring destination motivates the prerequisites
  • Exams are fine — they test specifics. They're the wrong starting point
  • Krashen analogy: fluency precedes grammar; intuition precedes rules

So, not Feynman style. It's gonna be my style, and I'm gonna combine all of the YouTube resources that I know that I like. So it's gonna be a mix of all of the best teachers on YouTube, but I'm gonna write the overall narrative, the path between them. So it's not Feynman style. So even starting from the fifth grade I would not at all hesitate to tell them about quarks and quark stars because that's the inspiring stuff that's gonna keep them asking questions. And in order to understand something as amazing as a quark star, or a Planck star, or how a solid thing can actually be a wave and two identical waves push each other while other waves don't — maybe that's not the most inspiring thing, but you can think of a lot of inspiring things. And then in order to understand those things, you're gonna have to start with something more simple. Then once the children know what the purpose is of understanding the simple things, they won't have so much of a problem with learning those simple things with high accuracy, which is what exams demand. Which is the same as language learning. Where examinations usually require that you know specific grammar rules. And that's okay because it's hard to measure how good a student is if you can't measure them specifically. So it makes sense that exams are specific, but it's not the best way to learn. And you need thousands of examples and thousands of hours of exposure, and then you can vaguely understand something and vaguely describe something. In the same way in physics, I suggest the deep forest approach, which is the same as the intuitive, high-input approach as Steven Krashen does — so that you can get a good intuition for physics. Only after you have a decent intuition, just like in language learning, you can then ask specific questions in order to solve very specific problems, but the context is gonna allow you to memorize those specific solutions much, much better.

So in the case of language learning, this would be that you understand the language more or less, but you don't know how something is spelled or what the correct order of the words is. But you know examples of people who say these things, but maybe you just can't remember. Then you find a rule and you remember. In the same way in physics, you learn in general the understanding of how physics works and what matter is and how it works and what the forces are and how they interact. And then you can ask the specific mathematical question of how much of this makes this, and what is energy specifically in other units.

You go into the deep forest, and you see amazing things, and you try to understand very complicated things. And you can get the idea. You don't need specifics. You can get the idea. You can answer some big questions, and you're trying to find kind of safe spots in the deep, dark forest. And with the deep forest, you don't have to dumb it down. You can show people that there's quantum mechanics and special relativity and that it changes things, but you don't have to explain it all — which is the problem with modern school curriculum is that they're so oriented towards exams that they're afraid to even talk about anything unless you can completely describe it and explain it. Which leaves the student with a lot of information that is not explained and contradictive. And so it leaves only one option, which is to memorize everything, which is not very interesting, which is why students don't want to learn.

The pedagogy in one line

Intuition first, precision later. The deep forest gives you something to be precise about.

Math pedagogy · Verbatim
Math as intuition first ▶
  • Multiplication is naturally 2D — forcing it onto a line breaks intuition
  • Quadratic formula should be derived by completing the square, not memorized
  • Pascal's triangle unifies binomial expansion, completing the square, derivatives
  • Derivative of x² as area growing on two sides — 2x falls out geometrically
  • Raising to a power decomposes into rotation and stretching
  • Zero and infinity: circular vs linear geometry reveal different relationships
▶ In depth · full monologue (verbatim, math section)

And even math I would teach in the same way. It might sound absurd that you can teach math in an abstract intuitive way, but math is very related to physics. So in the same way, a lot of math rules are just listed, and then you have to memorize them, and then you practice them. And I don't disagree with practicing math — a lot of algebra can be understood very quickly, but you need extremely high accuracy and extremely high speed with algebra for most of math and physics. So this requires pure practice. However, a lot of that algebra has discussions. You know — what a negative sign is. Why should we — is the line of numbers the only way to look at it? How is zero and infinity the same? How does a circular geometry and a linear geometry show similarities and differences between zero and infinity and circular motion? What is it to raise something to the power of something? How you can dismember that into rotations and stretching. What is multiplication — why is it more natural for multiplication to be multidimensional?

Which is why, for example, if you multiply two by two, you get four, which makes sense — you stretch it twice. But if you take two meters times two meters, you get four meters squared. It makes sense — two, you stretch it by two, you get four. But if you happen to measure the same line with different units, for example two thousand millimeters multiplied by two thousand millimeters, then suddenly you see that multiplication doesn't at all fit on a line. It's not natural for it. Suddenly you have two million, expanded by two million, so you get four trillion millimeters squared on a line — whereas if you expand it into an area, it looks natural. Two thousand by two thousand, you get a nice square. But if you try to squeeze that onto a line, the smaller your units, the more infinitely long it becomes. Which just shows you that your initial choice of units determines the length of the number, which is not symmetrical. Which means multiplication is only symmetrical in planes — not on lines.

So in the same way, you can ask what is a cosine and sine, what does it mean to raise to a power — and all of these questions can have discussions and analysis. And instead of just showing a thousand rules — one of the rules I was very surprised by in school, like how to multiply something like a squared plus b squared — if you try to square that, you see the pattern where you get Pascal's triangle. So this triangle appears in all sorts of places in math, which is not mentioned in school. And you can deduce logically what the values should be for each number, so you don't have to memorize the rules.

And secondly, when you try to find the solutions to a quadratic equation, they give you the determinant. They don't explain where the determinant comes from. So I've actually had a lot of fun trying to reinvent the determinant. And what I discovered was completing the square — which is trying to create a square from ax squared plus bx plus c makes it much easier. So it's just a tool which you — you connect the Pascal's triangle and the two-added-constant-squared and the derivative of the quadratic function. You connect all of those things, and you see why they're useful to one another. You can even ask deeper questions like, why does x squared expand like that? And you'd probably get a similar answer to the multiplication question — which is that the square of a number is not natural on a line. So if a quadratic equation was portrayed in a more natural environment, you would then see that it is increasing normally.

In the same way, when you're trying to find the derivative of something — again, they just give you the rules. But if you analyze it a bit, you can visualize it as the rate of change of an area, and then you see that if you want to calculate the rate of change of a square, it's actually the two sides that are increasing. So however you increase the square, it increases twice at that speed because it has two sides. So the derivative of x squared is 2x — and you derived it from geometry, not from a rule.

Pedagogical principle

Derive, don't memorize. Every "magic formula" in school math has a geometric or combinatorial picture underneath. The picture is the curriculum; the formula is the souvenir.

Pedagogy
Memorization — its role and its limits ▶
  • Memorization works short-term — Anki, spaced repetition are real tools
  • Long-term retention comes from natural re-exposure, not artificial drilling
  • Memorization creates hooks — anchors for future recognition
  • Without context, hooks attach to nothing and decay fast
  • With deep-forest context, the hook attaches to a rich web and persists
  • The fix is richer context, not better memorization tools
▶ In depth · full monologue (verbatim, memorisation section)

So this is just to show that mathematics can also be explained more intuitively, and only when you have the intuition, then you can use — I'm not against memorization. But memorization doesn't work long term. Even for subjects like Japanese, and for doctors, they use tools like Anki very heavily for just brutal memorization, which I've used myself. So I'm very familiar with memorization techniques and modern memorization tools. And I know that you can basically indefinitely remember something if you just space it out properly.

But in the end, your natural spaced repetition is gonna be your exposure to the field. So most memorization works like hooks. Either you don't have to do spaced repetition because you'll get it naturally once you encounter the subject in real life. But once you encounter it in real life, you need to be able to remember it so that you can recognize it and retrieve the memory in a real life scenario. Which is why initially, once you don't have enough context, rote memorization can be a great way to create hooks that allow you to notice things.

It's the same Steven Krashen idea about noticing — that a European person cannot learn the pitch accent of Japanese, but he can learn the pitch accent of Chinese. Because in Japanese, it's not necessary. So because it's not necessary, you have to add extra hooks for you to notice it. Once you add the hooks, you can notice it. But then if it has no use, the information should just die off. So if you think it's really useful and you're afraid it's gonna die off, the long term solution is to analyze the subject in a more complex way — because that's gonna create a lot of natural contextual hooks instead of artificial memorization technique hooks.

  • Implication for curriculum: don't try to outcompete Anki on flashcards. Compete on context-richness — which generates "natural contextual hooks instead of artificial memorization technique hooks".

Chain 1 · Feynman
Feynman Lecture 1 · Atoms in Motion ▶

The opening lecture of The Feynman Lectures on Physics. The most-cited science explanation of the 20th century. Maps onto Chain 1 (gases → atomic theory) but is foundational across the curriculum.

  • YouTube — Feynman Lecture 1: Atoms in Motion (search)Multiple uploads exist; pick the cleanest audio

Main ideas

  • The atomic hypothesis: "All things are made of atoms — little particles that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another." If only one sentence of scientific knowledge could be passed to a future civilization, this should be the one.
  • Three phases of matter as one mechanism: solids vibrate in place, liquids slide past each other, gases fly apart freely. Same atoms, different motion regimes.
  • Temperature = atomic motion. Heat is faster jiggling. Cooling stops at absolute zero — where motion reaches its minimum.
  • Pressure as collisions. Gas pressure on a wall is the cumulative impact of trillions of atomic hits per second.
  • Evaporation and condensation are the fastest molecules escaping a liquid's surface vs. slower atmospheric ones sticking back. Equilibrium when rates match.
  • Why ice floats: water atoms arrange into an open crystal lattice when frozen, taking up more space than in the disordered liquid.
  • Smell and taste are atoms reaching receptors — molecules of a substance physically travel through air or saliva to the nose or tongue.
  • Chemistry = atomic rearrangement. Reactions regroup atoms into new arrangements; nothing is created or destroyed. Direct preview of Lavoisier and mass conservation.
  • The scale problem. If an apple were enlarged to the size of the Earth, its atoms would be roughly the size of the original apple.
  • Inference power. An enormous amount of physical and chemical behaviour follows from a single atomic hypothesis — the lecture itself is a demonstration of how much one good idea can do.

Design
Visual differentiation — exam table is a grid, curriculum table is a card stack ▶
  • Exam table — dense data grid. Many columns (10 exams), many rows (~70 topics). Reads like a spreadsheet. Optimised for cross-reference, not for browsing.
  • Curriculum table — soft off-white panel containing white "lesson cards" stacked vertically. Bottom-up cards have a 3px blue left accent; top-down cards have a 3px purple accent. Hidden <thead>. Click rotates a chevron, expands the detail panel inline.
  • Click expands first, never auto-navigates. Even for lessons with their own page, clicking the card opens the detail panel; the page link is a prominent blue button inside that panel. Prevents accidental navigation when the reader just wants to peek at coverage.
  • Same data, two views. Both tables are rendered by JavaScript from examData8to10 + lectureData8to10 at the bottom of map.html. Editing a bullet in the data updates everywhere.

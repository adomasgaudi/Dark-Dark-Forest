Sciences before the scientific revolution

~2000 BCE → 1600 CE · 3600 years of all sciences, ancient observations, and the speculative frameworks that held it together

For roughly four thousand years before chemistry got its scientific revolution, humans wrestled with the world using the tools they had — sharp observation, philosophical reasoning, mathematical clarity where possible, and lots of carefully-tended speculation where not. The Babylonians catalogued the sky. The Egyptians wrote down medical recipes. The Greeks invented geometry, atomism, geocentric cosmology, the four humors, the four classical elements, and Aristotelian physics. The Romans copied and lost much of it. Indian mathematicians developed the decimal system and zero. Arabs preserved and extended everything from algebra to optics. Medieval Europeans rediscovered the Greeks via Arabic intermediaries, then started questioning them.

By 1600, on the eve of the scientific revolution, most of the "frameworks" on this chart were still alive. Geocentric astronomy was the official church position. Aristotelian physics was university orthodoxy. Galenic medicine was standard medical practice. Alchemy was still funded by kings. The decisive collapses — Galileo, Newton, Lavoisier, Wöhler, Pasteur — were all in the future.

Three charts, one pattern

This page, the chemistry table (1600 → 1900), and the physics table (1900 → present) tell the same story at three different magnifications. At every era, humans had a handful of cleanly understood things, a much larger pile of phenomena they were observing but not yet explaining, several speculative frameworks they used as scaffolding, and a list of open puzzles that drove the next generation.

What changes from chart to chart is the length of the bars. On this page, most bars run the full chart — humans wondered about fire and magnetism and gravity and disease for thousands of years before any of them got answers. By the chemistry chart, the time-from-question-to-answer compresses to centuries. By the physics chart, it compresses to decades, often years.

Read in reverse, the three charts say something more uncomfortable. The phenomena getting shorter answers are also the phenomena getting smaller and stranger. Ancient questions were "what is fire" — staring everyone in the face. Modern questions are "what is the muon's anomalous magnetic moment" — detectable only after billions of dollars of instrumentation. The mess shrinks, and the puzzles miniaturise to match.

The Cheat Sheet

Dear student. I know that you are very busy. So here, without a moment's delay, I will explain exactly what you need to pass your examination. In that examination, they will ask you:

Light and lenses. Explain the moon phases and solar eclipse. Explain how light reflects from objects and mirrors, and explain how light travels through different mediums. How do lenses in your glasses work? And the lenses in photography? And the lenses in telescopes? How do they work?

Sound and waves. Waves — you need to know what sound is and how to define it, what's an amplitude, what's a frequency. What are the units that describe these features? How do they move in space?

The shortcut. Of course I understand how incredibly busy you are, so here are all the formulas and all of the definitions. All you have to do is memorize them by heart, and hope that when you read the question, you will recognize immediately how to apply these formulas and definitions, and you will succeed, without fail, in getting the correct answer. But before I give you the formulas, I forgot one thing.

Atoms and radioactivity. If, unfortunately, you happen to get questions from a different topic, then you will also need to know what an atom is and what its composition is. How does it change? What properties does it have? What is the atomic number? And what is radioactive radiation?

Galaxies and stars. You might also get questions about the universe. What are galaxies and stars? How do stars live out their lives, and where do they get their energy?

Electricity. And of course you might get questions about electricity. How do objects obtain static electricity? What is the internal composition that leads to this? Calculate the current and the voltage and the resistance, and how those influence one another. You might also need to correctly identify the direction of the flow, as well as understand common electrical safety symbols. Draw your own schemes and apply Ohm's law when calculating combined resistances. So let me, of course, hand you the formulas and definitions for these questions as well.

— Everything below is genuinely correct and possibly even sufficient to pass. Whether it is sufficient to understand anything is a different question.

Topic I

Mechanical oscillations and waves — Sound

What sound is

Sound is a pressure wave. Waves we imagine as water waves, but in physics a wave is something that has a "wavy" property. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum, because there are no particles so nothing can do the waving.

Frequency and pitch

Frequency is how many waves pass per second. Higher frequency - more waves, we hear this as a higher pitch. Picture a plane propeler its starts of slow with low frequency and accelerates to high frequency. Its wing is producing the same waves but more times per second.

f = 1 / T
f = frequency in hertz (Hz)·T = period, the time for one full vibration, in seconds

Amplitude and loudness

Amplitude (amplus + tudo = large + state) is the size of each wave or in the case of of air - how strong the pressure wave is. Larger amplitude is heard as louder sound. We could measure that pressure by saying how much that sound wave increases normal air pressure, but then the unit would vary in daily life anywhere from 0.001 to 1000 and so a logorithmic measurement unit is used instead which varies usually only from 0 to 100. That unit is is decibels (dB). However because it is logorithmic, we have to understand the math to predict how it will change. A car might make a 70dB sound, while 10 cars would only make a 80dB sound. A 10x increase in sound intensity corresponds to a +10 dB increase.

Wave speed

The speed of a wave links its frequency and wavelength. Sound travels faster in denser, stiffer media — fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases. In air at room temperature it is about 343 m/s.

v = f · λ
v = wave speed (m/s)·f = frequency (Hz)·λ = wavelength (m)

Echo

An echo is sound reflected off a surface and returning to you. Because you hear it after a delay, you can find the distance to the surface if you know the speed of sound and the time for the round trip. The sound travels to the wall and back, so the distance is half the total path.

d = v · t / 2
d = distance to the surface (m)·v = speed of sound (m/s)·t = total time for the echo to return (s)

Types of sound by frequency

Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Below 20 Hz is infrasound (felt more than heard — earthquakes, large machinery). Above 20,000 Hz is ultrasound (used in medical imaging and by bats and dolphins).

Topic II

Light — Optics

Eclipses and shadows

Light travels in straight lines, so opaque objects cast shadows. A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, casting its shadow on us — the Moon blocks the Sun. A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. The Moon's phases are a separate effect: we see different fractions of its sunlit half depending on where it is in its orbit around us.

Law of reflection

When light hits a smooth surface, it bounces off at the same angle it arrived. Both angles are measured from the normal — an imaginary line perpendicular to the surface.

θincidence = θreflection
The angle coming in equals the angle going out, both measured from the normal.

Refraction and refractive index

When light passes from one medium into another (air into glass, say), it changes speed and bends. The refractive index measures how much a material slows light down. A higher index bends light more. This bending is why a straw looks broken in a glass of water.

n = c / v
n = absolute refractive index (no units)·c = speed of light in vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s)·v = speed of light in the material (m/s)

Lenses and image formation

A convex (converging) lens bends parallel light rays inward to a single focal point — used in magnifying glasses, cameras and telescopes. A concave (diverging) lens spreads rays outward. To draw the image, trace two rays from the top of the object: one parallel to the axis (which then passes through the focal point) and one straight through the centre of the lens (which is undeflected). Where they meet is where the image forms.

P = 1 / f
P = optical power of the lens in dioptres (D)·f = focal length in metres (m). A more powerful lens has a shorter focal length.
M = himage / hobject
M = magnification (how many times larger the image is)·h = height of image and object

Glasses for short and long sight

In short-sightedness (myopia), the eye focuses images in front of the retina; a concave lens spreads the light slightly so it focuses further back, onto the retina. In long-sightedness (hyperopia), the eye focuses behind the retina; a convex lens converges the light sooner, pulling the focus forward onto the retina.

Topic III

Structure of matter — Atoms and nuclei

The atom and its notation

An atom has a tiny dense nucleus of protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. The number of protons (the atomic number, Z) defines which element it is. The total number of protons plus neutrons is the mass number, A.

AZX
X = element symbol·Z = atomic number (protons)·A = mass number (protons + neutrons). Neutrons = A − Z.

Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons. They behave identically in chemistry but differ in mass and nuclear stability. Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are isotopes — both have 6 protons, but 6 and 8 neutrons respectively.

Radioactive radiation

Unstable nuclei shed energy and particles as radiation. There are three kinds: alpha (α) — a helium nucleus, heavy and easily stopped by paper; beta (β) — a fast electron, stopped by aluminium; gamma (γ) — high-energy light, needing thick lead or concrete. Penetrating power increases from alpha to gamma; ionising damage decreases.

Nuclear reactions and conservation laws

In any nuclear reaction, the total atomic number and the total mass number are conserved — they must balance on both sides of the equation. Fission splits a heavy nucleus into lighter ones (nuclear power, atomic bombs). Fusion merges light nuclei into a heavier one (the Sun, hydrogen bombs). Decay is a nucleus spontaneously emitting radiation to become more stable.

Radiation protection

Three ways to reduce exposure: increase distance from the source, reduce time near it, and add shielding (lead, concrete, water) between you and it.

Topic IV

The Universe and its evolution

What the universe is made of

On the largest scales, matter is organised into structures: galaxy clusters contain galaxies, which contain nebulae (clouds of gas and dust where stars are born) and stars. Our Sun is one star among hundreds of billions in the Milky Way galaxy.

Where stars get their energy

Stars shine by nuclear fusion — in the core, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium, releasing enormous energy. A small amount of mass is converted into energy in the process, following E = mc². This is the same reaction as a hydrogen bomb, held stable by the star's own gravity.

Stellar evolution by mass

A star's fate depends on its mass. Low-mass stars (like the Sun) swell into red giants, then gently shed their outer layers and leave behind a white dwarf. High-mass stars burn through their fuel quickly, explode as a supernova, and leave behind a neutron star or — if massive enough — a black hole. The heavy elements made in these explosions are scattered into space and become the raw material for new stars and planets.

Topic V

Electricity

Static electricity and charge

All matter contains positive protons and negative electrons. Objects become charged when electrons move between them — rub a balloon on hair and electrons transfer, leaving one object negative and the other positive. Like charges repel; opposite charges attract.

Current, charge and the quantities of a circuit

Current is the rate of flow of charge through a conductor. Voltage is the energy pushing that charge around the circuit. Resistance is how much the conductor opposes the flow.

I = Q / t
I = current (amperes, A)·Q = charge that flowed (coulombs, C)·t = time (s)

Ohm's law

The current through a conductor is proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance. This is the single most-used equation in the whole topic.

U = I · R
U = voltage (volts, V)·I = current (A)·R = resistance (ohms, Ω)

Electrical power and energy

Power is how fast electrical energy is used. Energy is power multiplied by time — and the kilowatt-hour (kWh) on your electricity bill is exactly this: a unit of energy, not power.

P = U · I
P = power (watts, W)·U = voltage (V)·I = current (A)
W = P · t
W = energy (joules, J — or kWh)·P = power (W or kW)·t = time (s or h)

Series and parallel resistance

In series (components in a single line), resistances add up directly. In parallel (components on separate branches), the combined resistance is smaller than any single branch, because charge has more paths to flow through.

Series: R = R₁ + R₂ + …
Total resistance is the sum.
Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
Add the reciprocals, then invert to get the total.

Current direction and circuit symbols

Conventional current flows from + to − around the circuit; electrons actually flow the opposite way, − to +. An ammeter measures current and is connected in series; a voltmeter measures voltage and is connected in parallel across a component. A fuse is a deliberate weak point that melts and breaks the circuit if the current grows dangerously high, protecting everything else.

Method

Why I wouldn't just memorise blindly

There are subjects in school exist for for a few different reasons, and you should know which is which.

The first is best one, its general education. Its understanding and comprehension of the world. This is the part that is the most interesting and the most useful and the longest lasting.

The second is that some of what you learn is the foundation for everything else. Learning how to measure sound waves is not really all that important to your understanding of nature — they are about waves. Once you understand a wave with percision, you have something that you can re-use in optics, in electromagnetism, in quantum mechanics. You learn the formula for one wave and you have learned a piece of the language nature uses everywhere.

The second is that some of what you learn is the working knowledge of a profession. Trigonometry, resistance in circuits, counting reactants and products in chemistry — these are drilled to precision because precision is exactly what an engineer or a chemist needs on a Tuesday morning.

So far so good. I am not arguing against learning these things, nor against drilling them, nor against being tested on them. I am arguing for something narrower. You should know why you're learning it.

The problem is that the drill was once a tool, sharpening something useful for a larger purpose. Over time standardzation of curicula and test and the tool quietly became the goal. Students now memorize formulas to pass the test that exists to verify they memorized the formulas. Why anyone started doing this in the first place — that part got lost.

What I want to do is put the tool back where it belongs. The drilling exercises and the uninteresting topics are a tool to understand and use everything else, so let's try and use it that way.

If memorizing forty formulas you don't understand felt unsatisfying — that was the point. The rest of this curriculum builds every one of them from scratch, so you never have to memorize blindly again.

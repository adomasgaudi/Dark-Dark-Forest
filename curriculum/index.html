The method

Intuition first.
Precision second.
Evidence-based throughout.

The curriculum is built on what learning research actually says — and on Krashen's insight that fluency precedes rules, applied to physics instead of language.

01
Deep forest first
Students aren't inspired by measuring the mundane. They're inspired by perception-altering ideas that feel within reach of understanding.
02
Derive, don't memorize
Every "magic formula" has a picture underneath. Physics laws, chemical conventions, and especially math formulas are usually handed down as tools for tasks the student never asked to do. Deriving them from first principles lights the belief that you could discover the laws of nature yourself — the way your predecessors did. Spaced repetition and memorization tools should complement understanding, not replace it.
03
One coherent story
Did Newton really invent calculus from scratch in one summer of quarantine? Did Einstein really write a year of genius papers out of nowhere? Taken without historical context, these stories carve a gap between "geniuses" and the rest of us. Complex ideas are always built from simpler ones. Newton built on Kepler, who got there only after 20 years of Tycho Brahe's data. Einstein built on Poincaré, who built on Lorentz, who built on Maxwell and Faraday. Genius comes in small iterations. The impact and the fame can be disproportionate.

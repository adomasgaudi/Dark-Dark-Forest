# Voice — turning the monologues into prose

A working guide for elevating Gaudi's spoken monologues into written pieces that earn the curriculum the kind of attention it deserves. The originals (preserved verbatim in `braindump/index.html`) are clear, honest, and full of insight. They are still spoken — they wander, hedge, repeat. Written prose can keep every idea and lose every hedge.

This document is **not** an edit. It's a sharpening guide. Pick what resonates.

---

## The literary inspirations

### The two poles (style)

**Richard Feynman** — joy, mischief, clarity. Makes you feel stupid and delighted at the same instant. He never lectures down to you; he discovers alongside you. Sentences are short and physical. *"There's plenty of room at the bottom."*

**Primo Levi** — chemist, Auschwitz survivor, author of *The Periodic Table*. One biography per element. Quiet, devastating, exact. He proves you can write about chemistry the way most writers can't write about anything. Weight without weight.

### The deep one (form)

**Jorge Luis Borges** — already wrote your curriculum, just called it fiction. Labyrinths, infinite libraries, mirrors, time loops. Every paragraph is a thought experiment. He never explains himself; the reader does the work and feels brilliant for it. The "deep forest" is a Borges concept.

### The supports

- **Carl Sagan** — cosmic scale + genuine awe. Never condescending. *Pale Blue Dot* is the benchmark for big-picture awe.
- **Vladimir Nabokov** — also a lepidopterist. Wrote about butterfly taxonomy with the same obsessive beauty as *Lolita*. Precision as aesthetics.
- **Christopher Hitchens** — surgical wit. The last great public intellectual.
- **George Orwell** — the clearest prose ever written. Six rules, all of them right.
- **Blaise Pascal** — physicist who wrote *Pensées*. Fragments. Existential dread about infinity. *"The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me."*

### The single pick

**Borges.** Because he wrote about labyrinths the way Gaudi talks about the deep forest. He would have written about quantum mechanics the way he wrote about mirrors: *as something that multiplies reality beyond what we can hold.*

---

## The voice equation

> Levi's weight  +  Feynman's joy  +  Borges's restraint

That's the curriculum's voice. Serious enough to mean it, playful enough to keep going, restrained enough that the reader does the discovering.

---

## Concrete moves to apply

1. **Cut the "so"s.** Spoken English uses "So" as a runway. Written English doesn't need one. Every paragraph that begins with "So" can start one word later.
2. **Replace hedging with claim.** "I would presume" → "I presume." "I'm not quite sure how to explain this yet" → cut, or write a footnote later.
3. **Compress repeated qualifiers.** "Very, very important" → "important." (Strunk: omit needless words.)
4. **End on the image, not the explanation.** The line *"the eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me"* is one sentence. After it, Pascal stops. Stopping is a craft.
5. **Use the concrete noun.** "Discoveries and explanations" → "the rule, the law, the symbol." Borges always picks the specific.
6. **Trust the reader.** Borges never says "this is interesting because…" He shows the labyrinth and walks away. Resist explaining your own metaphor.
7. **Vary sentence length brutally.** Short. Then a slightly longer one that develops the idea by adding one clause. Then a long sentence that lets the thought breathe across several beats, that piles up examples, that earns its length by ending on a hit.
8. **One striking image per paragraph.** Not three. The Medici funding Galileo. Aristotle going to check. Two million millimetres stretched on a line. Pick one and frame it.

## Edit pass · The Three Pillars

**Current opening (verbatim):**
> So you need the ability to abstract, to consider ideas in a more general matter, which then allows you to use those ideas in different frameworks, which is crucial because you cannot find generalized truths unless you try to combine different ideas from different frameworks.

**What's working:** the chain of dependencies (abstract → cross-framework → generalised truths). Real intellectual content.

**What to elevate:** four "frameworks" in two lines. Spoken language can repeat. Written language has to find the second word.

**Borges pass (one possible rewrite):**
> Science needs abstraction. Not because the world is abstract, but because no truth that holds in only one room is large enough to call a truth. The metalworker who knows his metals — who has built, in a lifetime at the forge, a private logic of how iron behaves — does not yet know science. He knows iron. Science begins the moment he wonders whether the same logic applies to glass.

Notice: same idea, half the words, one concrete image (the forge), a closing sentence that earns the period.

## Edit pass · The Natural Science Boundary

**Current opening (verbatim):**
> The last idea I want to mention is the idea of natural science, which I think is the idea that allowed abstract thought to be unhindered by religion.

**What's working:** the framing of "natural" as a deliberate boundary, not a metaphysical claim. This is the central move of the whole essay.

**What to elevate:** "the idea of … which I think is the idea that …" — three uses of "idea" in fifteen words. Also: don't apologise for the idea ("The last idea I want to mention"). Just say it.

**Pascal pass (a single fragment):**
> Natural science is an agreement, not a claim. The agreement is this: we will only play the game we can win. There are things outside it — perhaps higher powers, perhaps mysteries we cannot reach. We do not say they do not exist. We say only that we will not try to know them. We will spend our lives instead expanding the territory of the knowable. Two thousand years later we look up and the territory has eaten almost everything.

Notice: opens on a claim, develops it in three short lines, lands on an image (territory eating).

## Edit pass · Discrete vs Continuous (the Spherical Cow)

**Current opening (verbatim):**
> On top of all of this, I would like to add another important part of science, which is discrete thinking. You could think of thinking in two ways — one is more continuous, one is more discrete.

**What's working:** the binary frame (discrete vs continuous), and the move that connects matter (atoms) to ideas (atoms of thought).

**What to elevate:** "I would like to add another important part" — three filler words before the real noun arrives. Cut. Also: the abstract-to-concrete leap happens late. Lead with the example.

**Levi pass (chemistry-flavoured opening):**
> For most of human history, water was water — the same all the way down. You could divide a glass of it and divide the half-glass and divide that, and at no point did you expect to reach a thing that was no longer water. Then Dalton suggested otherwise. He said: there is a smallest piece. Below it, there is nothing called water. The same suggestion, made about thought, is just as unsettling. Is there a smallest idea? Below it, are there ideas at all?

Notice: opens on a concrete substance (water), reaches the abstraction through it, ends on a question — Borgesian, refusing to answer.

## Edit pass · The Deep Forest

**Current opening (verbatim):**
> So, not Feynman style. It's gonna be my style, and I'm gonna combine all of the YouTube resources that I know that I like.

**What's working:** the deep-forest metaphor itself, which is the single most quotable image in the whole project. Also the language-learning analogy — it is exactly correct and the reader doesn't see it coming.

**What to elevate:** the opening is housekeeping ("not Feynman style") — fine in conversation, kills momentum in writing. Lead with the image.

**Sagan/Borges pass (open on the forest, never explain it):**
> You take the student into the deep forest. You show them the strange things — the quark stars, the wave that is also a particle, the electron that needs to turn around twice before it turns around. You do not stop to explain. You let them see. The explaining will come later, in a different room, with a chalkboard, under fluorescent light, where exams live. But first, the forest.
>
> No school does this. Every school does the opposite: it forbids the forest until you can name every leaf in it.

Notice: opens on the action, paints the strange things in concrete language ("the electron that needs to turn twice before it comes home" — that's Borges), closes the paragraph on a hit, opens the next paragraph by indicting the alternative without raising its voice.

## A general principle

The monologues already contain the ideas. The edit's job is not to add depth — it's to remove softness. Every sentence that begins *"I think,"* *"I'm not sure but,"* *"I would presume,"* *"so,"* or *"basically"* should be tried without those words first. Most of them survive the cut and become stronger.

When in doubt, ask:

- Would Borges have stopped here? (Probably earlier.)
- Would Feynman have used this word? (Probably a shorter one.)
- Would Levi have explained this? (Probably not.)

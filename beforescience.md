# Why a historical, bottom-up approach

Hello, dear children. In this video series, I'll discuss the first-principles, bottom-up curriculum, which analyzes the history of science.

## [S1] History is a story

The reason I want to take this more historical approach is, first of all, because history is a story. It's a little more engaging than plain facts. And secondly, there are so many interesting stories and historical episodes which you don't necessarily need to know for physics or chemistry, but they're great just wonderful stories on their own. All of that gives you the context to anchor what you learn.

## [S2] Deriving everything from scratch

But most importantly: if you want to understand the subjects the way we're trying to in this series, you have to derive everything — to build it up from scratch. The problem is that if you already know the answer, it's very easy to leap over logical steps. It's easy to let faulty logic slip through when you don't need it yourself — which I'm sure you've experienced many times when explaining something you think you know to someone else, only to find out that you don't really understand it yourself. So by analyzing the history, we make sure we pay attention to the ideas that didn't work as well as the ideas that did work, to see just what kind of reasoning made the difference.

## [S3] The Big Bang as an example

An example of this would be the expanding universe and the Big Bang. A lot of explanations for why things happen aren't enough. You could explain the Big Bang and say that, based on the theory, the universe began from a singularity — an indescribably small point. And then, after only one quadrillion-quadrillionth of a second, it expanded to the size of the known universe and continued to expand. At first, atoms were created, and then later, that was created, and that was created. That's all good — but the theory is hard to understand. First of all, it's hard to believe. And even if you do believe it, you're forced to take everything as a fact. So it doesn't really give you a sense of how credible the information is, or where it's coming from.

But that also leaves other questions unanswered — why did the universe explode? What was it before the explosion? What kind of force expanded it? Something must have been very different in the beginning for it to behave in such a strange way. We don't see that kind of behavior around us.

This kind of explanation — of how or why something happened — can be misleading, because often we don't know why, and the history reflects that. When you're answering why or how, you're already implying that you know the answer, which isn't true in many cases. With the Big Bang, there are different theories, and inflation theory, although accepted, is nowhere near as well understood as something like quantum mechanics. Chemistry I could explain in quite a lot of detail without worrying that I made unfair assumptions, whereas in other fields you really have to make a lot of assumptions to explain something, because often there is no concrete explanation.

## [S4] The historical approach as honesty

The historical approach circumvents that. If you say, we don't need to know why or how the universe expanded — all we need to know is that the stars are moving in a way which suggests they are expanding away from each other, which presumes the universe was smaller before, which presumes it was hotter before. And because it was hotter, it must have had certain kinds of interactions. If that's true, we should be able to see other things that the heat of the early universe caused — and then you search for those things, and you find them. What are the chances that a made-up theory that's wrong somehow predicts new phenomena that exactly match? This historical explanation — we saw this, and then assumed that; we saw that, and assumed this — is much more honest to science, I think, than a hard explanation of what you think the universe exactly is.

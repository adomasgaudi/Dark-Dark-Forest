# Why a historical, bottom-up approach

Hello, dear children. In this video series, I'll discuss the first-principles, bottom-up curriculum, which analyzes the history of science.

## History is a story

The reason I want to take this more historical approach is, first of all, because history is a story. It's a little more engaging than plain facts. And secondly, there are so many interesting stories and events which you don't necessarily need to know for physics or chemistry, but they're interesting on their own. All of that gives you additional context in which to remember things.

## Deriving everything from scratch

But most importantly: if you want to understand the subjects the way we're trying to in this series, you have to derive everything — to build it up from scratch. The problem is that if you already know the answer, it's very easy to leap over logical steps, because you forgot the steps that would have been crucial if you didn't know the answer. So by analyzing the history, we make sure we're not missing any logical points.

## The Big Bang as an example

An example of this would be the expanding universe and the Big Bang. A lot of explanations for why things happen aren't sufficient. With the Big Bang, you could say the universe started from something very small and then exploded, and that's why we see the expansion of space. But that leaves other questions unanswered — why did the universe explode? What was it before the explosion? What kind of force expanded it? It seems to have been very different in the beginning to behave in such a strange way. We don't see that kind of behavior around us.

## "Why" and "how" imply you know the answer

This kind of explanation — of how or why something happened — can be misleading, because often we don't know why, and the history reflects that. When you're answering why or how, you're already implying that you know the answer, which isn't true in many cases. In the case of the Big Bang, there are different theories, and inflation theory, although accepted, is nowhere near as well understood as something like quantum mechanics. Chemistry I could explain in quite a lot of detail without worrying that I made unfair assumptions, whereas in other fields you really have to make a lot of assumptions to explain something, because often there is no concrete explanation.

## The historical approach as honesty

The historical approach circumvents that. If you say, we don't need to know why or how the universe expanded — all we need to know is that the stars are moving in a way which suggests they are expanding away from each other, which presumes the universe was smaller before, which presumes it was hotter before. And because it was hotter, it must have had certain kinds of interactions. If that's true, we should be able to see other things that the heat of the early universe caused — and then you search for those things, and you find them. What are the chances that a made-up theory that's wrong somehow predicts new phenomena that exactly match? This historical explanation — we saw this, and then assumed that; we saw that, and assumed this — is much more honest to science, I think, than a hard explanation of what you think the universe exactly is.
